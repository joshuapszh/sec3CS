Chapter 2: Probability

Probability Using Set Notations

If the sample space of an experiment has $m$ equally likely outcomes with $k$ ways for event $E$ to occur, then the probability of an event $E$ happening is:

$$P(E) = \frac{n(E)}{n(S)} = \frac{k}{m}$$

Recall that for any event $E$, $0 \le P(E) \le 1$. Therefore, $P(E) + P(E') = 1$.

Example - Tree Diagram
Three fair coins are tossed. Find the probability of getting (a) 3 heads, (b) at least one tail.

Solution (a): $P(3 \text{ heads}) = P(HHH) = \frac{1}{2} \times \frac{1}{2} \times \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{8}$

Solution (b): $P(\text{at least 1 tail}) = 1 - P(HHH) = 1 - \frac{1}{8} = \frac{7}{8}$

Addition Law and Mutually Exclusive Events

If two sets of events A and B cannot occur at the same time, then A and B are mutually exclusive. To put it simply, add when you see "or".

$$P(A \text{ or } B) = P(A) + P(B)$$

Multiplication Law and Independent Events

If two events A and B are independent, the occurrence of A does not affect the probability of B occurring. To put it simply, multiply when you see "and".

$$P(A \text{ and } B) = P(A) \times P(B)$$

Chapter 3: Statistics

Cumulative Frequency Table and Curve

Cumulative frequency is the running total of frequencies. The cumulative frequency of a value is obtained by adding up the frequencies which are less than or equal to that value.

Median, Quartiles, Percentiles, Range and IQR

  • Median (Q2): the middle value, $P_{50}$. Estimated using $\frac{1}{2} \times \text{total frequency}$.
  • Lower quartile (Q1): middle of the lower half, $P_{25}$. Estimated using $\frac{1}{4} \times \text{total frequency}$.
  • Upper quartile (Q3): middle of the upper half, $P_{75}$. Estimated using $\frac{3}{4} \times \text{total frequency}$.

Range = Largest value - Smallest value

Interquartile range (IQR) = Upper quartile (Q3) - Lower quartile (Q1)

Box-and-Whisker Plots

Summarises data using five numbers: minimum, lower quartile, median, upper quartile, and maximum. The box shows the middle 50% of the data.

Standard Deviation

Standard deviation measures the spread of a set of data from the mean. Interquartile range measures the spread from the median.

For Ungrouped Data

Formula 1: $\sigma = \sqrt{\frac{\sum (x - \bar{x})^2}{n}}$

Formula 2 (Easier): $\sigma = \sqrt{\frac{\sum x^2}{n} - \bar{x}^2}$

For Grouped Data

Formula 1: $\sigma = \sqrt{\frac{\sum f(x - \bar{x})^2}{\sum f}}$

Formula 2 (Easier): $\sigma = \sqrt{\frac{\sum fx^2}{\sum f} - \left(\frac{\sum fx}{\sum f}\right)^2}$

Chapter 4: Matrices

1. Introduction to Matrices

A matrix is a rectangular array of numbers. The order of a matrix tells us how many rows and columns it has. An $m \times n$ matrix has $m$ rows and $n$ columns.

Examples of Matrices Order Special Name
$$ \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix} $$ $2 \times 2$ Square matrix: Same number of rows and columns.
$$ \begin{pmatrix} 5 \\ 6 \\ 7 \end{pmatrix} $$ $3 \times 1$ Column matrix: Has only one column.
$$ \begin{pmatrix} 8 & 9 & 10 & 11 \end{pmatrix} $$ $1 \times 4$ Row matrix: Has only one row.
$$ I = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} $$ $2 \times 2$ Identity matrix: Diagonal elements = 1, others = 0.
$$ 0 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} $$ $2 \times 3$ Zero / Null matrix: All elements are zero.

2. Addition and Subtraction

We can only add or subtract matrices of the same order.

$$ \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix} \pm \begin{pmatrix} e & f \\ g & h \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} a \pm e & b \pm f \\ c \pm g & d \pm h \end{pmatrix} $$

3. Matrix Multiplication

Scalar Multiplication: If a matrix is multiplied by a scalar $k$, every element is multiplied by $k$.

$$ k \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} ka & kb \\ kc & kd \end{pmatrix} $$

Matrix by Matrix: Only possible if the number of columns in the left matrix equals the number of rows in the right matrix.

$$ A_{m \times n} \times B_{n \times r} = C_{m \times r} $$

Note: Matrix multiplication is generally NOT commutative (i.e., $AB \neq BA$).

Chapter 5: Vectors

1. Vectors in Two Dimensions

A vector has both magnitude and direction, whereas a scalar only has magnitude. Example: Distance is scalar, Displacement is vector.

Column Vectors & Magnitude: Written as $\begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix}$. The magnitude is $|\mathbf{a}| = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$.

Negative Vectors: $\vec{AB} = -\vec{BA}$. Same magnitude, opposite direction.

2. Addition and Subtraction of Vectors

Triangle Law: Join vectors tip-to-tail.

Parallelogram Law: Join vectors tail-to-tail, resultant is the diagonal.

By Column Vectors: $\begin{pmatrix} x_1 \\ y_1 \end{pmatrix} \pm \begin{pmatrix} x_2 \\ y_2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} x_1 \pm x_2 \\ y_1 \pm y_2 \end{pmatrix}$

3. Scalar Multiplication and Parallel Vectors

  • $\mathbf{a}$ is parallel to $\mathbf{b}$ if $\mathbf{a} = k\mathbf{b}$ for a scalar $k \neq 0$.
  • If points A, B, and C are collinear, then $\vec{AB} = k\vec{BC}$ and they share the common point B.

4. Position Vectors

The position vector of any point P is the vector from origin O to P. $\vec{OP} = \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix}$.

$$ \vec{PQ} = \vec{OQ} - \vec{OP} $$
